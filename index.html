PCB Fabrication — Technical Notes

What the reference demo actually shows

The Instagram reel,Independent, published workflows confirm the same underlying process: MOPA galvo lasers are used by makers to cut isolation lines and ablate copper on FR-4, but hole drilling and board routing in those documented workflows is done separately, on a CNC mill — not by the laser. That is consistent with laser-ablated holes being burned openings, not plated vias.

Cross-checked against a documented MOPA + CNC PCB workflow and a professional laser-PCB system's published spec sheet (min. insulating gap 0.025mm, ablation rate on 0.018mm copper) — see sources below.

Why MOPA over standard fiber

MOPA's adjustable pulse width is the real differentiator every vendor emphasised. It lets you control ablation depth precisely — enough to strip copper cleanly for trace isolation without charring or over-penetrating the FR-4 beneath. A fixed-pulse fiber laser is far more likely to scorch the substrate — the same documented workflow above notes a second cleanup pass is needed even on MOPA to avoid a charred finish.

Pros vs. traditional etch/CNC mill workflow

  • No wet chemistry (ferric chloride etc.) — safer for a student-heavy lab, no hazardous liquid waste.
  • Minutes-not-hours turnaround for single/double-sided isolation — good for iterative coursework prototyping.
  • One machine covers marking, engraving, and isolation — lower combined capex than separate systems.

Limitations to plan around

  • No plated through-holes / vias — single or double-sided prototype-grade boards only, not production-grade multilayer.
  • Minimum trace/space is spot-size limited — a comparable professional laser-PCB system publishes a 0.025mm minimum insulating gap and a 0.023mm beam diameter as its ceiling; hobby-grade MOPA setups in practice run coarser, closer to 0.1mm+, since isolation slots need to be wide enough not to bridge during soldering.
  • Fume extraction (HEPA + activated carbon) is non-negotiable, and is a recurring consumable cost, not a one-time add-on.
  • No solder mask or silkscreen step — those stay manual or need a separate process.
  • Galvo optics and lens need periodic recalibration — this is exactly why local (Karnataka) service response matters for a heavily-used student machine.

Rough annual throughput (planning estimate, not a guarantee)

The 15–30 min/board figure assumes a 100mm × 100mm single-sided FR-4 coupon — a standard quarter-Eurocard prototype size, not a full production panel. Larger or double-sided boards will take proportionally longer.

At that size — about 15 boards/day is realistic in an 8-hour academic day. Over ~200 teaching days that's ~2,500–3,000 boards/year for coursework alone; extending into project and incubation hours (more days, longer shifts) could push this toward 5,000+/year. Actual numbers will swing with board complexity and operator turnaround.

Access model — students free, startups cost-recovery

The common, sustainable pattern at university innovation labs: free at point-of-use for enrolled students on coursework/capstone (funded as academic infrastructure, tracked via a booking log), a modest per-hour or per-board cost-recovery rate for KLE-incubated startups (Startup Street / C-SHInE ventures) to cover consumables and uptime, and a full commercial rate for any external/walk-in use. This keeps the machine self-sustaining without turning it into a profit centre, and gives us a clean basis to renegotiate the institutional discounts vendors have already floated.
